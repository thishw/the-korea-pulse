South Korea's top economic post is changing hands after roughly eight months, and the incoming nominee has already flagged the limits of the government's most-discussed capital markets fix. Lee Hyung-il, nominated to succeed Koo Yun-cheol as deputy prime minister and minister of finance and economy, said on September 2 that he is weighing whether revising inheritance and gift tax law can, by itself, resolve the practice of "stock price suppression" — the long-standing complaint that controlling families have an incentive to keep listed valuations low ahead of succession to shrink their tax bills. A day later, the outgoing minister publicly endorsed him.

## A Handover With No Daylight Between the Principals

Speaking in Sejong on September 3, Koo described his designated successor as "very excellent" and said Lee understands the philosophy of the Lee Jae Myung administration well. The warm handoff matters because the ministry Lee would inherit is itself new: the administration split the former Ministry of Economy and Finance in 2026, carving out a separate planning-and-budget ministry and leaving the Ministry of Finance and Economy as the finance-focused successor. Koo became its minister at the start of January 2026, making him the reorganized ministry's inaugural chief — and Lee, if confirmed, its first leadership transition.

The ministry has kept to its calendar through the transition. It has published its 2027 budget compilation, and it confirmed that Koo departed as scheduled for the G20 finance ministers' meeting rather than standing down early.

## The Inheritance Tax Question Behind the "Korea Discount"

Lee's September 2 remarks go to the center of the government's capital markets agenda. Reform advocates argue that Korea's inheritance and gift tax regime — under which a lower share price at the moment of succession means a smaller tax liability — gives controlling shareholders a structural reason to tolerate, or even engineer, depressed valuations. That incentive is frequently cited as one driver of the persistent discount at which Korean equities trade relative to global peers.

By openly questioning whether a tax-code amendment alone can dissolve that incentive, Lee signaled that he views the problem as broader than a single statute. He did not, in the reported remarks, commit to an alternative package. But the framing suggests the nominee sees suppressed valuations as the product of several interacting rules — taxation, governance, and shareholder protections — rather than one lever that can be pulled in isolation. How he elaborates on that view during his confirmation hearing will be the first concrete test of whether the new ministry shifts emphasis on the issue.

## Why the Institutional Backdrop Raises the Stakes

Korea's economic ministries have been reorganized repeatedly at moments of policy stress, and the current structure is the latest turn in that cycle. A standalone Ministry of Finance ran the country's financial administration from 1948 to 1994, alongside the Economic Planning Board created in 1961 under Park Chung Hee to centralize control of industry. The two merged in 1994, were pulled apart again in the 1998 reorganization that followed the Asian financial crisis, and were recombined in 2008 into the Ministry of Economy and Finance — the body that operated for 18 years before the 2026 split.

Each restructuring redistributed authority over budgeting, taxation, and financial policy, and each new configuration took time to settle. That history is why a smooth first succession matters: Lee would take over a ministry still defining its remit against a new budget authority, while carrying a capital markets debate in which he has already staked out a carefully hedged position. The endorsement from Koo removes one source of friction. The harder questions — starting with what, beyond tax law, the government is prepared to change — now move to the confirmation process.
